               Families of Classification Boundaries:
    Locally Adaptive Support Vector Machines Against Matched Global References,
           with an Exact Statement of When Local Adaptation Cannot Help

                                        Luciano Silva Alarco
                               Pontificia Universidad Católica del Perú
                                    ORCID 0000-0002-3395-3129

                Working draft v0.1 — research line SVMF001 — September 30, 2026



                                               Abstract
     We revisit a paused research line on “families of classification boundaries”: variants of the
     support vector machine whose decision boundary adapts locally, compared against global
     references. The original variants are not available, so this draft reconstructs a self-contained
     version of the question with four small, transparent families—a localised (kNN) linear SVM,
     a cell-partitioned linear SVM, an RBF-SVM with a positive-definite variable-bandwidth
     kernel, and a soft mixture of local linear SVMs—each containing a global reference (linear or
     RBF-SVM) as a special case of its own hyper-parameter grid. Two contributions are made.
     First, an exact statement of when local adaptation cannot help: if the reference’s function
     class already contains the Bayes rule, localisation can only act on the estimation error, and
     for local models fitted on cells chosen independently of the labels the expected risk equals a
     mixture of the reference’s learning curve at binomially thinned sample sizes, hence is never
     smaller when that curve is non-increasing and strictly larger when it is strictly decreasing.
     In the Gaussian location model this is closed-form for the nearest-centroid threshold: with
     n = 320 points and four cells the excess risk is multiplied by 4.08. Second, a controlled
     comparison with a criterion of added value fixed before running: paired improvement over
     the best global reference with a bootstrap 95% interval excluding zero on a majority of
     6 data sets (three real, three synthetic regimes), repeated stratified cross-validation, and
     robustness under 20% label noise and 25% subsampling. No family met the majority criterion.
     Across 24 data-set/family cells of the main condition, 8 intervals lie below zero and 1 above
     (VB-RBF-SVM on Gaussian, linear); the latter is a +0.75 percentage-point gain against a
     reference chosen by inner cross-validation on the very data set where the exact statement
     applies, and it disappears against the better of the two global references (−0.37 [−1.25,
     +0.25]). In the regime designed to favour local adaptation (spatially varying scale and density)
     the variable-bandwidth family matched but did not beat the RBF-SVM. The finding of the
     original line—that exploratory improvements depend on the regime and the required added
     value was not supported—is reproduced with a stated protocol, and no general superiority
     in robustness or accuracy is claimed. All computations run in about 231 CPU-seconds with
     a fixed seed.


1    Introduction
A support vector machine with a fixed kernel draws one boundary for the whole input space [5,
14, 16]. Several proposals let the boundary adapt to where the query point lies: train the classifier
only on the neighbours of the query [2, 4, 15, 21], split the space into cells with one model each,
let the kernel width follow the local density as in variable-bandwidth density estimation [1, 19],
or blend local linear classifiers with soft weights [10]. Each of these can, in principle, fit a
boundary whose curvature or scale varies across the space; each also fits its local model on fewer
points than the global reference.


                                                    1
    The research line SVMF001 studied such “families of classification boundaries” and recorded,
in its public summary, that the exploratory improvements depended strongly on the regime and
that the final comparison did not support the required added value over comparable references,
with no claim of general superiority in robustness or classification. The concrete variants and
the regimes are not available to us. This draft therefore does two things, in the same spirit and
at a deliberately modest scale.

 1. It states and proves, in a precise but limited form, when local adaptation cannot help
    (Section 3): when the reference’s class already contains the Bayes rule, and the local models
    are fitted on cells chosen independently of the labels, the expected risk of the localised
    rule is a mixture of the reference’s learning curve at smaller sample sizes. One instance is
    computed exactly.

 2. It reconstructs the comparison as a controlled experiment with a criterion of added value
    fixed in advance (Section 4), four small families that each contain a global reference as
    a special case, six data sets grouped into named regimes, and two robustness conditions
    (Section 5).

Scope. The families are reconstructions, not the original variants; the data sets are small
(all subsampled to at most 400 points, for a total budget of a few CPU-minutes); the exact
statement covers partition-based localisation and not neighbourhood-based localisation, which is
only illustrated. Section 6 lists every claim with its status.


2     Setting and the four families
2.1   Risk, excess risk, learning curves
Let (X, Y ) be a random pair in X × {−1, +1}, X ⊆ Rd , with distribution P , η(x) = P(Y =
1 | X = x). The risk of a classifier f : X → {−1, +1} is R(f ) = P(f (X) ̸= Y ), the Bayes rule
is f ∗ (x) = sign(2η(x) − 1) with risk R∗ , and the excess risk is E(f ) = R(f ) − R∗ ≥ 0 [7]. A
learning rule A maps a finite labelled sample S of any size m ≥ 0 to a classifier A(S) (with a
fixed default for m = 0). Its learning curve is RA (m) = E R(A(Sm )) for Sm an i.i.d. sample of
size m from P .
Definition 2.1 (Approximation and estimation error). For a class F of classifiers and fˆ ∈ F
fitted on a sample,
                       E(fˆ) = inf R(f ) − R∗ + R(fˆ) − inf R(f ) .

Both terms are non-negative; the approximation term is zero if and only if F contains a Bayes
rule (up to null sets).

2.2   Global references and locally adaptive families
All families below are fitted on standardised inputs. The two references are the soft-margin linear
SVM f (x) = sign(⟨w, x⟩ + b) and the RBF-SVM with kernel kγ (x, z) = exp(−γ∥x − z∥2 ) [3, 5],
both with the penalty parameter C and, for the RBF, γ chosen by inner cross-validation from
grids given in Section 4. The four locally adaptive families are:

(a) kNN-SVM (localised linear SVM) [2, 21]. For a query x let Nk (x) be the k training points
    nearest to x. If all their labels coincide, predict that label; otherwise predict with a linear
    SVM (fixed C) trained on Nk (x) alone. The value k = “all” gives the global linear SVM
    with the same C.

Remark 2.3 (Nesting). Every local family contains a global reference in its own grid (k = all,
M = 1, β = 0), and in the experiment ties in the inner cross-validation are broken towards that
member. A local family can therefore fall below its own global member only through selection
noise, and can rise above the best global reference only through genuine adaptation. The kernel
prefactor in Lemma 2.2 is what makes √ Ka a valid kernel; in dimension d it down-weights pairs
with mismatched bandwidths by (2 ab/(a + b))d/2 , which is a strong penalty when d is large.
Dropping the prefactor is not an option: the matrix [exp(−∥xi − xj ∥2 /(ai + aj ))] is not positive
semidefinite in general.


3    When local adaptation cannot help
Lemma 3.1 (Only the estimation term is available). [proved here, elementary] Let Fref contain a
Bayes rule. For any classifier ĝ (local or not), E(ĝ) ≥ 0 = approximation(Fref ). Consequently
a locally adaptive procedure can improve on a reference fitted in Fref only by having a smaller
estimation term, never by reducing the approximation term, which is already zero.

Proof. Immediate from Definition 2.1 and E ≥ 0.

   The lemma is trivial but it locates the question: when the Bayes boundary is a hyperplane
and the reference is a linear SVM, or more generally when the reference class is rich enough (a
universal kernel [11, 17]), local adaptation is a bet that fitting on fewer points estimates better.
The next proposition makes this exact for partition-based localisation.

4                            Experimental protocol and predefined criterion
Data sets. Six binary tasks (Table 3), grouped into three named regimes fixed before running:
real (wine, class 0 against the rest; breast cancer; digits, even against odd, a task with several
modes per class), linear (the Gaussian location model of Section 3 in d = 10, δ = 3.29, Bayes
error 0.0500, so that the linear reference contains the Bayes rule), and multiscale, designed to
favour local adaptation: two-scale moons (100 points of the standard moons and 300 points
of a copy shrunk by 4 and displaced, so that scale and density both vary) and a two-scale
checkerboard (100 points on 2 × 2 cells of side 1 and 300 points on 4 × 4 cells of side 12 ). Breast
cancer and digits are subsampled (stratified) to 300 and 400 points and digits are reduced to 16
principal components fitted on the training fold; this subsampling is part of the compute budget
and is stated as such.

Resampling and tuning. Main condition: 2 repetitions of stratified 5-fold cross-validation
(10 outer folds). Two robustness conditions with 5 outer folds each: noise20, in which 20% of
the training labels of each fold are flipped at random (test labels clean), and sub25, in which

Reference and paired comparison. The best global reference in an outer fold is whichever
of the linear and RBF-SVM has the higher inner-cross-validation score in that fold; its test
accuracy is the reference value, so the choice uses no test data. For each family and data set we
record the paired difference (family minus reference) in each outer fold and a percentile bootstrap
95% interval of the mean difference over folds (B = 2000) [9]. Outer folds of a cross-validation
are not independent, so these intervals are descriptive rather than exact [8, 12]; with 10 folds
they are also wide, which works against detecting small effects in either direction.

Predefined criterion of added value. A family has added value if (A) its mean paired
improvement over the best global reference is positive with a bootstrap interval excluding zero
on a majority of the 6 data sets in the main condition; or, failing that, (B) there is a named
regime on every data set of which (A) holds, and no data set outside the regime shows an interval
entirely below zero. Robustness is reported separately as the accuracy under the two conditions
and the paired differences there. The criterion, the regimes, the grids and the seed were fixed
before the run; one sensitivity check added after seeing the results is labelled as post hoc in
Section 5.

5.3       Verdict under the predefined criterion
Table 10 applies the criterion of Section 4 literally. No family meets the majority clause (A).
The regime clause (B) is met, by the letter, by one family in one regime: VB-RBF-SVM in the
linear regime, which consists of a single data set. We report this as the rule requires and then
say why it is not added value in the sense intended: the regime is the one in which Section 3
shows that no reduction of excess risk is available, the gain vanishes against the better global
reference (Table 6), and a one-data-set regime is too weak a base for a named-regime claim.
The flaw is in our criterion, which should have required at least two data sets per regime; we
record it rather than repair it after the fact. In the multiscale regime, for which the family was
designed, the outcome is a tie with the RBF-SVM.

6      What can be claimed
Limitations. The families are reconstructions chosen for transparency, not the strongest
versions in the literature: the local SVMs are linear with a fixed C, the cell and anchor partitions
come from a single k-means run, the kNN-SVM’s inner validation is subsampled, and the
variable-bandwidth exponent takes three values. The data sets are small by design (a total
budget of about 231 CPU-seconds), so the comparison speaks to the regime of a few hundred
training points, where the variance mechanism of Section 3 is strongest and local methods
are at a disadvantage; with much larger samples the balance may differ, and nothing here
says otherwise. The bootstrap intervals treat the outer folds as exchangeable, which they are
not. Proposition 3.2 covers partitions independent of the labels; data-dependent partitions and
neighbourhoods are only illustrated. The criterion’s regime clause admitted a single-data-set
regime, a design flaw reported above.

Next steps for the research line. (a) A lower bound for neighbourhood-based localisation:
for the kNN-localised linear rule in the Gaussian model, bound the conditional excess risk at a
query by the local sample size, which would extend Proposition 3.2 to the kNN-SVM. (b) Re-run
the comparison at n in the thousands with the same criterion (repaired to require two data sets
per regime) to see whether the multiscale tie turns into a gain for the variable-bandwidth family.
(c) Compare the variable-bandwidth kernel with the simpler alternative of a global RBF-SVM on
density-normalised inputs, which may capture the same effect without the dimension-dependent
prefactor of Lemma 2.2. (d) If the original variants of the line are recovered, insert them into
this protocol unchanged.

Details. Standardisation and, for digits, PCA are fitted on the training fold only; the robustness
conditions are applied to the training fold after preprocessing. Inner folds are stratified with
a fold-specific seed shared by all families. The kNN-SVM uses Euclidean neighbours in the
standardised space; pure neighbourhoods vote without fitting. Cell-SVM and LLSVM share one
k-means partition per inner fold and M (one initialisation, fixed seed). The variable-bandwidth
family reuses the RBF-SVM’s inner scores for β = 0, which are identical by construction.
Bootstrap intervals are percentile intervals over 2000 resamples of the fold-wise paired differences.
The exact sums of Section 3 use scipy.stats.binom and scipy.stats.norm; the Monte Carlo
check evaluates the exact risk of each fitted threshold rather than an empirical test error.

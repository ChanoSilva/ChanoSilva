\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Research line EQO001 asked whether a formal relationship between equilibrium operators, variational inequalities and welfare criteria could yield results beyond the existing ones; its public record calls the formulation reviewed a combination of known components without an independent theorem. This note records that assessment. With equilibria the solutions of $\VI(F,K)$ on a closed convex set $K$ and welfare a $C^1$ functional on $K$, we prove the four classical facts on which any such relationship rests: potentials, monotonicity, the equilibrium--welfare gap (Pigou, $4/3$) and the first-order coincidence whose repair is marginal-cost pricing. The one small addition characterises the cone $\Lam(x^*)$ of welfare weights for which a Nash equilibrium maximises $\sum_i\lambda_iu_i$: a closed convex cone inside a first-order cone $\Lone(x^*)$ built from $F(x^*)$, the normal cone and the marginal externalities, with equality under joint concavity; $(F,K)$ alone never determines it. Two examples show that $\Lam(x^*)$ need not be polyhedral and that $\Lam\ne\Lone$ in two-commodity routing with affine costs. Numerical checks on \RndTotalInst{} random monotone games confirm equality in every tested direction of every concave instance and certify strict inclusion in at least \RndNoncWitPct\% of the non-concave instances with a non-trivial cone. No new equilibrium concept is offered.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

The variational-inequality formulation of equilibrium is one of the unifying ideas of applied mathematics: a Wardrop traffic equilibrium~\citep{wardrop1952}, a Nash equilibrium of a concave game~\citep{nash1951,rosen1965} and a Walrasian or spatial price equilibrium can all be written as: find $x^*$ in a closed convex set $K$ with $\ip{F(x^*)}{x-x^*}\ge 0$ for all $x\in K$~\citep{hartmanStampacchia1966,kinderlehrer1980,dafermos1980,harkerPang1990,nagurney1993,facchineiPang2003}. Welfare is an optimisation problem over the same $K$, and the temptation that motivated research line EQO001 is obvious: to look for a ``formal relationship'' between the inequality and the optimisation problem from which welfare statements about equilibria would follow.

The public record of the line is candid: the formulation reviewed combined known components without establishing an independent theorem, and the line was paused. This note discharges the line's objective in the only way that seems honest to us: by writing down, with complete proofs where they are short, what is a theorem in this area, what is a definition in disguise, and what remains open. We add one small result on the set of welfare weights that make a given equilibrium optimal; Remark~\ref{rem:novelty} states which parts of it are standard and which are, as far as we could check, only repackaged here.

\paragraph{Contributions and scope.} Section~\ref{sec:setting} fixes the setting; Section~\ref{sec:classical} proves the classical results; Section~\ref{sec:when} gives the first-order characterisation of welfare-optimal equilibria and marginal-cost pricing; Section~\ref{sec:cone} contains the welfare-weight cone (Theorem~\ref{thm:cone}), the impossibility statement (Proposition~\ref{prop:notdet}) and four exact examples, two of them negative (Examples~\ref{ex:nonpoly},~\ref{ex:routing}); Sections~\ref{sec:exp}--\ref{sec:limits} report the numerical checks, the status of every claim, limitations and two open questions. Everything is finite-dimensional and deterministic; every number in the text is produced by the scripts of Appendix~\ref{app:repro} with fixed seeds.

\section{Setting}\label{sec:setting}

Throughout, $K\subseteq\R^n$ is a nonempty closed convex set, $U\supseteq K$ an open set and $F\colon U\to\R^n$ a continuous map, the \emph{equilibrium operator}. We write $\ip{\cdot}{\cdot}$ for the Euclidean inner product.

\begin{definition}[Variational inequality, normal cone]\label{def:vi}
$\VI(F,K)$ is the problem of finding $x^*\in K$ such that $\ip{F(x^*)}{x-x^*}\ge 0$ for all $x\in K$; its solution set is $\SOL(F,K)$. The \emph{normal cone} of $K$ at $x\in K$ is $\NC_K(x)=\{v\in\R^n:\ip{v}{y-x}\le 0\ \forall y\in K\}$, a closed convex cone, equal to $\{0\}$ when $x$ is an interior point. By definition,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vi-normal}
x^*\in\SOL(F,K)\iff -F(x^*)\in\NC_K(x^*).
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Monotonicity]
$F$ is \emph{monotone} on $K$ if $\ip{F(x)-F(y)}{x-y}\ge 0$ for all $x,y\in K$; \emph{strictly monotone} if the inequality is strict for $x\ne y$; \emph{$\mu$-strongly monotone} if $\ip{F(x)-F(y)}{x-y}\ge\mu\|x-y\|^2$ with $\mu>0$; and \emph{$L$-Lipschitz} if $\|F(x)-F(y)\|\le L\|x-y\|$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Game]\label{def:game}
A \emph{game} has $N$ players, strategy sets $K_i\subseteq\R^{n_i}$ closed and convex, $K=K_1\times\cdots\times K_N$, and payoffs $u_i\colon U\to\R$ of class $C^1$ on an open $U\supseteq K$, with $u_i(\cdot,x_{-i})$ concave on $K_i$ for every $x_{-i}$. A \emph{Nash equilibrium} is $x^*\in K$ with $u_i(x^*)\ge u_i(y_i,x^*_{-i})$ for all $i$ and $y_i\in K_i$. The \emph{pseudo-gradient} (equilibrium operator) is $F(x)=\big(-\nabla_{x_1}u_1(x),\dots,-\nabla_{x_N}u_N(x)\big)$, where $\nabla_{x_i}$ is the gradient with respect to player $i$'s own variables. The \emph{marginal externality} of player $j$ on player $i\ne j$ at $x$ is $\nabla_{x_j}u_i(x)\in\R^{n_j}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Nash equilibria are VI solutions]\label{lem:nash}
In the setting of Definition~\ref{def:game}, $x^*$ is a Nash equilibrium if and only if $x^*\in\SOL(F,K)$. \status{classical, \citealp{rosen1965}; \citealp{facchineiPang2003}}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For a concave differentiable function $\phi$ on a convex set $C$, $z$ maximises $\phi$ over $C$ iff $\ip{\nabla\phi(z)}{y-z}\le 0$ for all $y\in C$: necessity because $t\mapsto\phi(z+t(y-z))$ is defined on $[0,1]$ and has right derivative $\ip{\nabla\phi(z)}{y-z}$ at $0$, which must be $\le 0$; sufficiency because $\phi(y)\le\phi(z)+\ip{\nabla\phi(z)}{y-z}$. Apply this to $\phi=u_i(\cdot,x^*_{-i})$ on $K_i$: $x^*$ is Nash iff $\ip{-F_i(x^*)}{y_i-x_i^*}\le 0$ for all $i$ and $y_i\in K_i$. Summing over $i$ gives $\ip{F(x^*)}{y-x^*}\ge 0$ for every $y\in K$; conversely, choosing $y=(y_i,x^*_{-i})$ in the VI recovers the $i$-th inequality.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Nonatomic routing]\label{def:wardrop}
A network has edges $E$, commodities $k=1,\dots,m$ with demands $d_k>0$ and path sets $P_k$, $P=\bigsqcup_k P_k$. A path flow $f\in\R^P_+$ is feasible if $\sum_{p\in P_k}f_p=d_k$ for each $k$; $K$ is the set of feasible flows, a product of scaled simplices. Edge flows are $f_e=\sum_{p\ni e}f_p$, edge costs $c_e\colon\R_+\to\R_+$ are continuous and nondecreasing, path costs are $c_p(f)=\sum_{e\in p}c_e(f_e)$, and the equilibrium operator is $F(f)=(c_p(f))_{p\in P}$. A \emph{Wardrop equilibrium} is a feasible $f^*$ such that, for every $k$ and $p,q\in P_k$ with $f^*_p>0$, $c_p(f^*)\le c_q(f^*)$. The \emph{social cost} is $C(f)=\sum_{p}f_pc_p(f)=\sum_e f_ec_e(f_e)$, and welfare is $W=-C$.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Normal cone of a face of a simplex]\label{lem:simplex}
Let $\Delta=\{x\in\R^P_+:\sum_{p\in P_k}x_p=d_k,\ k=1,\dots,m\}$ and $x\in\Delta$ with support $S=\{p:x_p>0\}$. Then
\[
\NC_\Delta(x)=\{v: \exists\,\gamma\in\R^m \text{ with } v_p=\gamma_k \text{ for } p\in S\cap P_k,\ v_p\le\gamma_k \text{ for } p\in P_k\setminus S\}.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For $p,q\in P_k$ with $q\in S$ and $t>0$ small, $y=x+t(e_p-e_q)\in\Delta$, so $v\in\NC_\Delta(x)$ implies $v_p\le v_q$; exchanging $p,q$ when both are in $S$ gives equality on $S\cap P_k$, say $v_p=\gamma_k$, and $v_p\le\gamma_k$ off $S$. Conversely, if $v$ has this form and $y\in\Delta$, then $\ip{v}{y-x}=\sum_k\big(\sum_{p\in P_k}v_py_p-\gamma_kd_k\big)\le\sum_k\big(\gamma_k\sum_{p\in P_k}y_p-\gamma_kd_k\big)=0$, using $v_p\le\gamma_k$ on $P_k$ and $y\ge 0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Wardrop equilibria are VI solutions]\label{lem:wardrop}
$f^*$ is a Wardrop equilibrium iff $f^*\in\SOL(F,K)$ with $F=(c_p)_p$. \status{classical, \citealp{smith1979}; \citealp{dafermos1980}}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
By \eqref{eq:vi-normal} and Lemma~\ref{lem:simplex}, $f^*\in\SOL(F,K)$ iff for each $k$ there is $\gamma_k$ with $c_p(f^*)=-\gamma_k$ on used paths of $P_k$ and $c_p(f^*)\ge-\gamma_k$ on unused ones, which is Wardrop's condition.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Welfare functionals]
A \emph{welfare functional} is a $C^1$ function $W\colon U\to\R$ to be maximised over $K$. For a game, the \emph{weighted welfare} with weights $\lambda\in\R^N_+$ is $W_\lambda=\sum_i\lambda_iu_i$; $\lambda=\mathbf 1$ is the utilitarian criterion. For routing, $W=-C$.
\end{definition}

\section{The classical results, with proofs}\label{sec:classical}

\subsection{Potentials}

\begin{proposition}[Poincar\'e lemma on a convex set]\label{prop:poincare}
Let $U$ be open and convex and $F\colon U\to\R^n$ of class $C^1$ with symmetric Jacobian $DF(x)$ at every $x$. Fix $x_0\in U$ and set $P(x)=\int_0^1\ip{F(x_0+t(x-x_0))}{x-x_0}\,\dd t$. Then $P\in C^2(U)$ and $\nabla P=F$. \status{classical; proved here}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
With $\gamma_t=x_0+t(x-x_0)$ and $h=x-x_0$, differentiation under the integral sign and the symmetry $\partial_iF_j=\partial_jF_i$ give
$\partial_iP(x)=\int_0^1\big[F_i(\gamma_t)+t\sum_j\partial_jF_i(\gamma_t)h_j\big]\dd t=\int_0^1\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\big[tF_i(\gamma_t)\big]\dd t=F_i(x)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Equilibria of gradient operators]\label{thm:potential}
Suppose $F=\nabla P$ on $U\supseteq K$ with $P\in C^1(U)$. Then
\[
\SOL(F,K)=\{x\in K:\ip{\nabla P(x)}{y-x}\ge 0\ \forall y\in K\},
\]
the set of stationary points of $P$ over $K$; it contains $\arg\min_KP$, and equals it when $P$ is convex on $K$. If $P$ is strictly convex, $\SOL(F,K)$ has at most one point. \status{classical: \citealp{beckmann1956} for routing, \citealp{mondererShapley1996} for potential games; proved here}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The first identity is Definition~\ref{def:vi} with $F=\nabla P$. If $x$ minimises $P$ over $K$ then, as in the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:nash}, the right derivative of $t\mapsto P(x+t(y-x))$ at $0$ is nonnegative, which is the stationarity inequality. If $P$ is convex and $x$ is stationary, $P(y)\ge P(x)+\ip{\nabla P(x)}{y-x}\ge P(x)$ for all $y\in K$. If $P$ is strictly convex its minimiser over the convex set $K$ is unique.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The three classical instances]\label{rem:instances}
(a) \emph{Routing}~\citep{beckmann1956}. For continuous $c_e$, $P(f)=\sum_e\int_0^{f_e}c_e(s)\,\dd s$ is $C^1$ with $\partial P/\partial f_p=\sum_{e\in p}c_e(f_e)=c_p(f)$ by the fundamental theorem of calculus (no differentiability of $c_e$ is needed), and convex when the $c_e$ are nondecreasing; Wardrop equilibria are the minimisers of $P$, and the edge flows are unique when all $c_e$ are strictly increasing. Note that $P\ne C$: the potential integrates the cost, the social cost multiplies it; this is the whole of Section~\ref{sec:classical}.3.
(b) \emph{Concave games}~\citep{rosen1965}. Lemma~\ref{lem:nash} and Theorem~\ref{thm:mono}(a) give uniqueness under Rosen's diagonal strict concavity, which for unit weights is strict monotonicity of the pseudo-gradient.
(c) \emph{Potential games}~\citep{mondererShapley1996}. A $C^1$ game is an exact potential game with potential $\Pi$ iff $F=-\nabla\Pi$; its equilibria are the stationary points of $-\Pi$ over $K$, its maximisers when $\Pi$ is concave. The potential is bookkeeping, not welfare: in general $\Pi\ne\sum_iu_i$ (Example~\ref{ex:cournot}(i) is a potential game whose equilibrium maximises no weighted welfare).
\end{remark}

\subsection{Monotonicity: uniqueness, existence and a solver}

\begin{lemma}[Projection]\label{lem:proj}
Let $\Pi_K$ be the Euclidean projection onto $K$. Then $z=\Pi_K(y)$ iff $z\in K$ and $\ip{y-z}{x-z}\le 0$ for all $x\in K$; $\Pi_K$ is nonexpansive; and for every $\gamma>0$, $x\in\SOL(F,K)$ iff $x=\Pi_K(x-\gamma F(x))$. \status{classical, \citealp[Chapter~I]{kinderlehrer1980}}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The first claim is Theorem~\ref{thm:potential} for the strictly convex $P=\tfrac12\|y-\cdot\|^2$; adding the characterisations at $z=\Pi_Ky$ and $z'=\Pi_Ky'$ gives $\|z-z'\|^2\le\ip{y-y'}{z-z'}$, hence nonexpansiveness; and $x=\Pi_K(x-\gamma F(x))$ iff $\ip{-\gamma F(x)}{y-x}\le 0$ for all $y\in K$, which is the VI.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Monotone operators]\label{thm:mono}
(a) If $F$ is strictly monotone on $K$, $\SOL(F,K)$ has at most one point. (b) If $F$ is $\mu$-strongly monotone and $L$-Lipschitz on $K$, $\SOL(F,K)$ is a singleton $\{x^*\}$, and for $0<\gamma<2\mu/L^2$ the iteration $x_{t+1}=\Pi_K(x_t-\gamma F(x_t))$ satisfies $\|x_{t+1}-x^*\|\le q\|x_t-x^*\|$ with $q=\sqrt{1-2\gamma\mu+\gamma^2L^2}<1$; for $\gamma=\mu/L^2$, $q=\sqrt{1-\mu^2/L^2}$. \status{classical, \citealp{kinderlehrer1980,facchineiPang2003}; proved here}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
(a) If $x,y\in\SOL(F,K)$ then $\ip{F(x)}{y-x}\ge 0$ and $\ip{F(y)}{x-y}\ge 0$; adding, $\ip{F(x)-F(y)}{x-y}\le 0$, so $x=y$ by strict monotonicity. (b) Let $T(x)=\Pi_K(x-\gamma F(x))$. By nonexpansiveness,
$\|Tx-Ty\|^2\le\|(x-y)-\gamma(F(x)-F(y))\|^2=\|x-y\|^2-2\gamma\ip{F(x)-F(y)}{x-y}+\gamma^2\|F(x)-F(y)\|^2\le q^2\|x-y\|^2$,
and $q<1$ iff $\gamma<2\mu/L^2$. Banach's fixed-point theorem on the complete set $K$ gives a unique fixed point, which is the unique VI solution by Lemma~\ref{lem:proj}, and the contraction estimate is the stated rate.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:hs}
For merely continuous $F$ on compact convex $K$, existence is the Hartman--Stampacchia theorem (\citealp[Lemma~3.1]{hartmanStampacchia1966}), obtained from Brouwer's theorem applied to $T$; we do not reproduce it. All solvers in Section~\ref{sec:exp} are the iteration of Theorem~\ref{thm:mono}(b) with $\gamma=\mu/L^2$, so each computed equilibrium comes with a uniqueness proof and a residual certificate.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Equilibrium is not welfare: Pigou and the price of anarchy}

\begin{example}[Pigou]\label{ex:pigou}
Two parallel links, unit demand, $c_1\equiv 1$ and $c_2(x)=x^d$, $d\ge 1$~\citep{pigou1920}. Every user on link 2 pays $1\le c_1$, so $f^*=(0,1)$ is a Wardrop equilibrium with $C(f^*)=1$, and it is the only one: a feasible flow with $f_1>0$ has $f_2<1$, so its users on link 1 pay $1>f_2^d$. The social cost of $(1-x,x)$ is $1-x+x^{d+1}$, minimised at $x=(d+1)^{-1/d}$ with value $1-\tfrac{d}{d+1}(d+1)^{-1/d}$. For $d=1$ the optimum is $(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$ with cost $\tfrac34$ and ratio $\tfrac43$; the ratio tends to infinity with $d$, since $(d+1)^{-1/d}\to 1$. The computed values are in Figure~\ref{fig:poa} and \path{results/tables_traffic.md}.
\end{example}

\begin{theorem}[Price of anarchy for affine costs]\label{thm:poa}
If every $c_e(x)=a_ex+b_e$ with $a_e,b_e\ge 0$, then for every Wardrop equilibrium $f$ and every feasible $f'$, $C(f)\le\tfrac43C(f')$. The constant is attained in Example~\ref{ex:pigou} with $d=1$. \status{classical, \citealp{roughgardenTardos2002}; the proof below follows \citealp{correa2004}}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:wardrop}, $\sum_pc_p(f)(f'_p-f_p)\ge 0$; regrouping by edges, $C(f)=\sum_ec_e(f_e)f_e\le\sum_ec_e(f_e)f'_e$. For each edge,
$c_e(f_e)f'_e=c_e(f'_e)f'_e+a_e(f_e-f'_e)f'_e\le c_e(f'_e)f'_e+\tfrac14a_ef_e^2\le c_e(f'_e)f'_e+\tfrac14c_e(f_e)f_e$,
since $y\mapsto a_e(f_e-y)y$ is maximised at $y=f_e/2$ and $a_ef_e^2\le(a_ef_e+b_e)f_e$. Summing, $C(f)\le C(f')+\tfrac14C(f)$.
\end{proof}

\section{When an equilibrium is a welfare maximiser}\label{sec:when}

\begin{proposition}[Necessary and sufficient first-order conditions]\label{prop:nec}
Let $x^*\in\SOL(F,K)$ and let $W$ be differentiable at $x^*$.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em,label=(\alph*)]
\item If $x^*\in\arg\max_KW$ then $\nabla W(x^*)\in\NC_K(x^*)$ and $-F(x^*)\in\NC_K(x^*)$. If $W$ is concave on $K$, the converse holds: $x^*\in\arg\max_KW$ iff $\nabla W(x^*)\in\NC_K(x^*)$.
\item If $x^*$ is an interior point of $K$, (a) forces $F(x^*)=0=\nabla W(x^*)$. If $\NC_K(x^*)=\R_+\nu$ is a ray and $F(x^*)\ne 0$, (a) forces $\nabla W(x^*)=-\theta F(x^*)$ for some $\theta\ge 0$.
\item (Standard form.) Let $K=\{x\in\R^n_+:Ax=b\}$ with $A\in\R^{m\times n}$ and let $S$ be the support of $x^*$. If $x^*$ is an equilibrium and a $W$-maximiser, there are $y,z\in\R^m$ with
\begin{gather*}
F_S(x^*)=(A^{\!\top}y)_S,\qquad F_j(x^*)\ge(A^{\!\top}y)_j\quad(j\notin S),\\
\nabla_SW(x^*)=(A^{\!\top}z)_S,\qquad \partial_jW(x^*)\le(A^{\!\top}z)_j\quad(j\notin S).
\end{gather*}
In particular, on a single simplex ($A=\mathbf 1^{\!\top}$) both $F(x^*)$ and $\nabla W(x^*)$ are constant on the support (hence parallel on it when both are non-zero), and each satisfies Wardrop's principle with respect to its own coordinates.
\item If $F$ is strictly monotone, $W$ is strictly concave and both $\SOL(F,K)$ and $\arg\max_KW$ are nonempty, then $\SOL(F,K)=\arg\max_KW$ iff the unique equilibrium satisfies $\nabla W(x^*)\in\NC_K(x^*)$; otherwise the two sets are disjoint.
\end{enumerate}
\status{proved here; elementary}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
(a) The first-order necessary condition for a maximum of a differentiable function over a convex set (proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:nash}) is $\ip{\nabla W(x^*)}{y-x^*}\le 0$ for all $y\in K$, i.e. $\nabla W(x^*)\in\NC_K(x^*)$; the second membership is \eqref{eq:vi-normal}; sufficiency under concavity is the same argument as in Theorem~\ref{thm:potential}. (b) For interior $x^*$, $\NC_K(x^*)=\{0\}$. If $\NC_K(x^*)=\R_+\nu$, write $-F(x^*)=\alpha\nu$ and $\nabla W(x^*)=\beta\nu$ with $\alpha,\beta\ge 0$; $F(x^*)\ne 0$ gives $\alpha>0$ and $\theta=\beta/\alpha$. (c) The tangent cone of $K$ at $x^*$ is $\{h:Ah=0,\ h_j\ge 0\ (j\notin S)\}$ and its polar, by Farkas' lemma~\citep{rockafellar1970}, is $\NC_K(x^*)=\{A^{\!\top}w-v:w\in\R^m,\ v\ge 0,\ v_S=0\}$. Apply this to $-F(x^*)$ (with $y=-w$) and to $\nabla W(x^*)$ (with $z=w$). (d) Both sets are singletons by Theorem~\ref{thm:mono}(a) and strict concavity; apply (a).
\end{proof}

Proposition~\ref{prop:nec} is a statement about first-order conditions and nothing more: it says that an equilibrium can be a welfare optimum only if the two problems happen to share their KKT point, and it gives no mechanism producing that coincidence. The one mechanism that always works is to change the operator.

\begin{corollary}[Marginal-cost pricing and the toll set]\label{cor:toll}
Let $C\in C^1(U)$ be a cost to be minimised over $K$ and define the \emph{toll field} $\tau=\nabla C-F$. Then
\[
\SOL(F+\tau,K)=\{x\in K:-\nabla C(x)\in\NC_K(x)\}\supseteq\arg\min_KC,
\]
with equality when $C$ is convex on $K$, and at most one point when $C$ is strictly convex; the tolled operator is a gradient field with potential $C$. For separable routing costs with $c_e\in C^1$, $\tau_p(f)=\sum_{e\in p}f_ec_e'(f_e)$, the Pigou--Knight--Beckmann marginal-cost toll. More generally, for a target $\bar x\in K$ the set of constant toll vectors $t\in\R^n$ with $\bar x\in\SOL(F+t,K)$ is the translated cone $-F(\bar x)-\NC_K(\bar x)$, polyhedral when $K$ is; choosing in it the toll closest to a reference vector is an inverse optimisation problem in the sense of \citet{ahujaOrlin2001}, and for routing it is the toll-set description of \citet{hearnRamana1998}. \status{classical, \citealp{pigou1920,knight1924,beckmann1956,hearnRamana1998}; proved here}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
$F+\tau=\nabla C$, so the first statement is Theorem~\ref{thm:potential} with $P=C$. For routing, $\partial C/\partial f_p=\sum_{e\in p}\big(c_e(f_e)+f_ec_e'(f_e)\big)$, whence $\tau_p$. Finally $\bar x\in\SOL(F+t,K)$ iff $-F(\bar x)-t\in\NC_K(\bar x)$ by \eqref{eq:vi-normal}.
\end{proof}

\section{The cone of welfare weights}\label{sec:cone}

Fix a game as in Definition~\ref{def:game} and a Nash equilibrium $x^*$. Write $u=(u_1,\dots,u_N)\colon K\to\R^N$ and $G(x^*)=Du(x^*)\in\R^{N\times n}$ for the Jacobian of the payoff vector, whose $i$-th row is $\nabla u_i(x^*)^{\!\top}$ (all variables, own and others'). Define
\[
\Lam(x^*)=\{\lambda\in\R^N_+: x^*\in\arg\max_KW_\lambda\},\qquad
\Lone(x^*)=\{\lambda\in\R^N_+: G(x^*)^{\!\top}\lambda\in\NC_K(x^*)\}.
\]
For a set $D\subseteq\R^N$, $D^\circ=\{\lambda:\ip{\lambda}{d}\le 0\ \forall d\in D\}$ is its polar cone.

\begin{theorem}[Welfare-weight cone]\label{thm:cone}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em,label=(\alph*)]
\item $\Lam(x^*)=\R^N_+\cap\{u(x)-u(x^*):x\in K\}^\circ$. In particular $\Lam(x^*)$ is a closed convex cone.
\item $\Lam(x^*)\subseteq\Lone(x^*)$, and $\Lone(x^*)$ is a closed convex cone, polyhedral whenever $K$ is polyhedral.
\item If every $u_i$ is concave on $K$ (jointly in all variables), then $\Lam(x^*)=\Lone(x^*)$. More generally, equality holds whenever $W_\lambda$ is concave on $K$ for every $\lambda\in\Lone(x^*)$.
\item (Block form.) $\lambda\in\Lone(x^*)$ iff for every player $j$
\begin{equation}\label{eq:block}
-\lambda_jF_j(x^*)+\sum_{i\ne j}\lambda_i\nabla_{x_j}u_i(x^*)\in\NC_{K_j}(x_j^*).
\end{equation}
Since $-F_j(x^*)\in\NC_{K_j}(x_j^*)$ already, a sufficient condition is that the $\lambda$-weighted externality $\sum_{i\ne j}\lambda_i\nabla_{x_j}u_i(x^*)$ lies in $\NC_{K_j}(x_j^*)$ for every $j$; when $x_j^*$ is interior to $K_j$, \eqref{eq:block} says exactly that this weighted externality vanishes.
\item $\Lam(x^*)\ne\{0\}$ implies that $x^*$ is weakly Pareto efficient in $K$ (no $x\in K$ with $u_i(x)>u_i(x^*)$ for all $i$); under the concavity of (c), the converse holds.
\end{enumerate}
\status{proved here; (a) and (e) are the standard scalarisation argument of multi-objective optimisation \citep{geoffrion1968,ehrgott2005}, and $\Lam(x^*)$ is the cell at $u(x^*)$ of the weight-set decomposition studied for linear problems by \citet{bensonSun2000}}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
(a) $\lambda\in\Lam(x^*)$ iff $\lambda\ge 0$ and $\ip{\lambda}{u(x)-u(x^*)}\le 0$ for all $x\in K$, which is the stated intersection; polar cones and the orthant are closed convex cones.
(b) If $x^*$ maximises the differentiable $W_\lambda$ over $K$, the first-order condition gives $\nabla W_\lambda(x^*)=\sum_i\lambda_i\nabla u_i(x^*)=G(x^*)^{\!\top}\lambda\in\NC_K(x^*)$. $\Lone(x^*)$ is the preimage of the closed convex cone $\NC_K(x^*)$ under a linear map, intersected with $\R^N_+$; when $K$ is polyhedral so is $\NC_K(x^*)$, hence so is $\Lone(x^*)$.
(c) For $\lambda\ge 0$, $W_\lambda$ is concave when the $u_i$ are, and the first-order condition is then sufficient (Proposition~\ref{prop:nec}(a)); the general statement is the same argument.
(d) For a product set, $v\in\NC_K(x^*)$ iff $v_j\in\NC_{K_j}(x_j^*)$ for each $j$: taking $y\in K$ that differs from $x^*$ in block $j$ only shows necessity, and summing the block inequalities shows sufficiency. Block $j$ of $G(x^*)^{\!\top}\lambda$ is $\sum_i\lambda_i\nabla_{x_j}u_i(x^*)=\lambda_j\nabla_{x_j}u_j(x^*)+\sum_{i\ne j}\lambda_i\nabla_{x_j}u_i(x^*)$ and $\nabla_{x_j}u_j(x^*)=-F_j(x^*)$. The sufficient condition follows because $\NC_{K_j}(x_j^*)$ is a convex cone containing $-F_j(x^*)$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:nash}; for interior $x_j^*$, $\NC_{K_j}(x_j^*)=\{0\}$ forces $F_j(x^*)=0$ and then \eqref{eq:block} reads $\sum_{i\ne j}\lambda_i\nabla_{x_j}u_i(x^*)=0$.
(e) If $\lambda\in\Lam(x^*)\setminus\{0\}$ and $u(x)>u(x^*)$ componentwise, then $\ip{\lambda}{u(x)}>\ip{\lambda}{u(x^*)}$, a contradiction. Conversely, under concavity the set $A=u(K)-\R^N_+$ is convex; weak efficiency means $u(x^*)+\varepsilon\mathbf 1\notin A$ for every $\varepsilon>0$, so $u(x^*)$ is a boundary point of $A$ and a supporting hyperplane gives $\lambda\ne 0$ with $\ip{\lambda}{a}\le\ip{\lambda}{u(x^*)}$ for all $a\in A$; since $u(x^*)-\R^N_+\subseteq A$, $\lambda\ge 0$, and $a=u(x)$ gives $\lambda\in\Lam(x^*)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[What is standard and what is not]\label{rem:novelty}
The scalarisation in (a) and (e) is textbook, and the set of weights under which a given point maximises the weighted sum is a standard object of multi-objective optimisation (the weight-set decomposition, polyhedral in the linear case \citep{bensonSun2000}). What we did not find in the literature we consulted, and claim only as packaging, is the reading of \eqref{eq:block}: the cone is cut out by the equilibrium face (through $\NC_{K_j}(x_j^*)$ and $F_j(x^*)$, both determined by $(F,K)$) and by the marginal externalities $\nabla_{x_j}u_i(x^*)$, which are \emph{not} determined by $(F,K)$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:notdet}). The interior case of (d) is also the mechanism behind the theorem of \citet{dubey1986} that Nash equilibria of smooth games are, in general position, Pareto-inefficient: at an interior $x^*$, any $\lambda\in\Lone(x^*)\setminus\{0\}$ must annihilate every weighted externality, which a generic game does not allow; Example~\ref{ex:cournot}(i) is an instance.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[$(F,K)$ does not determine welfare]\label{prop:notdet}
Let the $u_i$ be continuous on $K=\prod K_i$ with $K_i$ compact, and let $x^*$ be a Nash equilibrium. Define $v_i(x_{-i})=\max_{y_i\in K_i}u_i(y_i,x_{-i})$ and $u_i'=u_i-v_i$. Then the game $(u_i')$ has the same best-response correspondences and the same Nash equilibria as $(u_i)$; wherever $\nabla_{x_i}u_i$ exists, $\nabla_{x_i}u_i'=\nabla_{x_i}u_i$, so the two games have the same equilibrium operator; and $x^*$ maximises every $W'_\lambda=\sum_i\lambda_iu_i'$, $\lambda\in\R^N_+$, over $K$: $\Lam'(x^*)=\R^N_+$. Consequently welfare optimality of an equilibrium is not a property of $(F,K)$: every pair $(F,K)$ that arises from a game is compatible with $\Lam(x^*)=\R^N_+$, and the pair of Example~\ref{ex:cournot}(i) is also compatible with $\Lam(x^*)=\{0\}$. \status{proved here; the function $\sum_i(v_i-u_i)$ is the Nikaid\^o--Isoda gap function~\citep{nikaidoIsoda1955}}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
$v_i$ does not depend on $x_i$, so subtracting it changes neither $\arg\max_{y_i}u_i(y_i,x_{-i})$ nor $\nabla_{x_i}u_i$. By construction $u_i'\le 0$ on $K$, and $u_i'(x^*)=0$ because $x_i^*$ is a best response to $x^*_{-i}$; hence $W'_\lambda\le 0=W'_\lambda(x^*)$ for every $\lambda\ge 0$. The last sentence combines this with Example~\ref{ex:cournot}(i), where $\Lam(x^*)=\{0\}$.
\end{proof}

The modified game need not be $C^1$ ($v_i$ is in general only directionally differentiable), so it may fall outside Definition~\ref{def:game}; $\Lam'(x^*)$ is then understood in the exact sense of Theorem~\ref{thm:cone}(a), which needs no gradients. When the best response $b_i(x_{-i})$ is unique for every $x_{-i}$ (e.g.\ $u_i$ strictly concave in $x_i$), Danskin's theorem~\citep{danskin1966} gives $v_i\in C^1$ with $\nabla v_i(x_{-i})=\nabla_{x_{-i}}u_i(b_i(x_{-i}),x_{-i})$; the modified game is then in the class of Definition~\ref{def:game} and, since $b_i(x^*_{-i})=x^*_i$, it has \emph{zero marginal externalities at $x^*$}: $\nabla_{x_j}u_i'(x^*)=0$ for $j\ne i$. By Theorem~\ref{thm:cone}(d), $\Lone'(x^*)=\R^N_+$ as well, which is the first-order reason for the proposition.

\begin{example}[Two players, exact cone]\label{ex:wedge}
Let $K=[0,1]^2$, $\alpha>1$, $\beta_1,\beta_2\ge 0$, and
\[
u_1(x)=\alpha x_1-\tfrac12x_1^2-\beta_1x_2,\qquad u_2(x)=\alpha x_2-\tfrac12x_2^2-\beta_2x_1 .
\]
Each player's action harms the other linearly. The equilibrium operator is $F(x)=(x_1-\alpha,x_2-\alpha)$, with Jacobian $I$, so $F$ is $1$-strongly monotone and $1$-Lipschitz and does not depend on $\beta$; the unique equilibrium is $x^*=(1,1)$, at which $\NC_K(x^*)=\R^2_+$ and $F(x^*)=(1-\alpha)\mathbf 1$. Here $G(x^*)=\begin{psmallmatrix}\alpha-1&-\beta_1\\-\beta_2&\alpha-1\end{psmallmatrix}$, so by \eqref{eq:block}
\[
\Lone(x^*)=\{\lambda\ge 0:\ (\alpha-1)\lambda_1\ge\beta_2\lambda_2,\ (\alpha-1)\lambda_2\ge\beta_1\lambda_1\}.
\]
Both payoffs are jointly concave (Hessians $-\operatorname{diag}(1,0)$ and $-\operatorname{diag}(0,1)$), so $\Lam(x^*)=\Lone(x^*)$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:cone}(c). Direct check: $W_\lambda$ is separable, $x_1=1$ maximises $\lambda_1(\alpha x_1-\tfrac12x_1^2)-\lambda_2\beta_2x_1$ on $[0,1]$ iff its derivative at $1$, $\lambda_1(\alpha-1)-\lambda_2\beta_2$, is $\ge 0$, and symmetrically. Thus the cone is a wedge, non-trivial iff $\beta_1\beta_2\le(\alpha-1)^2$ and two-dimensional iff the inequality is strict; for $\alpha=2$, $\beta_1=\beta_2=\tfrac12$ it is $\{\tfrac12\lambda_2\le\lambda_1\le2\lambda_2\}$ (Figure~\ref{fig:wedge}): the corner equilibrium is welfare-optimal exactly when neither player is weighted more than twice the other. Two games with $\beta$ and $\beta'$ have the same $F$ and different cones, which is Proposition~\ref{prop:notdet} in miniature; here the modification of that proposition is explicit and $C^1$: $v_1(x_2)=\alpha-\tfrac12-\beta_1x_2$, so $u_1'=\alpha x_1-\tfrac12x_1^2-\alpha+\tfrac12$ no longer depends on $\beta$ and the modified cone is $\R^2_+$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Cournot duopoly]\label{ex:cournot}
Let $u_i(q)=q_i(a-q_1-q_2)$ on $K=[0,1]^2$; $\partial^2u_1/\partial q_1^2=-2<0$, so Lemma~\ref{lem:nash} applies, and $F(q)=(2q_1+q_2-a,\ q_1+2q_2-a)$ has symmetric part $\begin{psmallmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{psmallmatrix}\succ0$. (i) For $a=1$ the equilibrium $q^*=(\tfrac13,\tfrac13)$ is interior, $F(q^*)=0$, and $\partial u_1/\partial q_2=-q_1^*\ne 0$: by Theorem~\ref{thm:cone}(d) the interior condition fails for every $\lambda\ne 0$, so $\Lam(q^*)=\{0\}$ (Dubey's inefficiency, Remark~\ref{rem:novelty}). Indeed the collusive $(\tfrac14,\tfrac14)$ gives each firm $\tfrac18>\tfrac19$. This is an exact potential game with $\Pi(q)=a(q_1+q_2)-q_1^2-q_2^2-q_1q_2$, whose maximiser is the equilibrium: the potential is not a welfare functional. (ii) For $a=4$ the equilibrium is the corner $(1,1)$, $F(q^*)=(-1,-1)$, $G(q^*)=\begin{psmallmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{psmallmatrix}$ and $\Lone(q^*)=\{\lambda_1=\lambda_2\ge 0\}$, a ray. The payoffs are not jointly concave, so Theorem~\ref{thm:cone}(c) is silent; but $W_{(1,1)}(q)=(q_1+q_2)(4-q_1-q_2)$ is concave, so $\Lam(q^*)=\Lone(q^*)$ nevertheless. Concavity is sufficient, not necessary.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Three players: a non-polyhedral cone]\label{ex:nonpoly}
Let $K=[0,1]^3$ and
\[
u_1=x_1,\qquad u_2=x_2,\qquad u_3=x_3-\tfrac12x_1^2+\tfrac14x_1+\tfrac12x_1x_2-\tfrac12x_2 .
\]
Each payoff is linear, hence concave, in the player's own variable, so the game is in the class of Definition~\ref{def:game}; $F\equiv(-1,-1,-1)$, $x^*=(1,1,1)$ is the unique Nash equilibrium and $\NC_K(x^*)=\R^3_+$. Since $\nabla u_3(x^*)=(-\tfrac14,0,1)$, $G(x^*)^{\!\top}\lambda=(\lambda_1-\tfrac14\lambda_3,\lambda_2,\lambda_3)$ and $\Lone(x^*)=\{\lambda\ge 0:\lambda_1\ge\tfrac14\lambda_3\}$. We compute $\Lam(x^*)$ exactly. On $\{\lambda_3=0\}$, $W_\lambda=\lambda_1x_1+\lambda_2x_2$ is maximised at $x^*$, so $\R^2_+\times\{0\}\subseteq\Lam(x^*)$; as $\Lam(x^*)$ is a cone it suffices to fix $\lambda_3=1$. Then $W_\lambda$ is increasing in $x_3$, so $x_3=1$, and affine in $x_2$, so the maximum over $x_2$ is attained at $x_2\in\{0,1\}$. For $x_2=1$, $W_\lambda=\tfrac12+\lambda_2+(\lambda_1+\tfrac34)x_1-\tfrac12x_1^2$ is maximised on $[0,1]$ at $x_1=1$ iff $\lambda_1\ge\tfrac14$, with value $W_\lambda(x^*)=\lambda_1+\lambda_2+\tfrac34$. For $x_2=0$, $W_\lambda=1+(\lambda_1+\tfrac14)x_1-\tfrac12x_1^2$ has maximum $1+\tfrac12(\lambda_1+\tfrac14)^2$ if $\lambda_1\le\tfrac34$ (at $x_1=\lambda_1+\tfrac14$) and $\lambda_1+\tfrac34\le W_\lambda(x^*)$ otherwise. Hence $\lambda\in\Lam(x^*)$ iff $\lambda_1\ge\tfrac14$ and $\lambda_1+\lambda_2+\tfrac34\ge1+\tfrac12(\lambda_1+\tfrac14)^2$, i.e.
\[
\Lam(x^*)\cap\{\lambda_3=1\}=\big\{\lambda_1\ge\tfrac14,\ \lambda_2\ge\tfrac12\big(\tfrac34-\lambda_1\big)_+^2\big\}.
\]
Its boundary contains the parabolic arc $\lambda_2=\tfrac12(\tfrac34-\lambda_1)^2$, $\tfrac14\le\lambda_1\le\tfrac34$, which no finite union of segments contains (a line meets a parabola in at most two points); since the slice of a polyhedral cone by a hyperplane is a polyhedron, $\Lam(x^*)$ \emph{is not polyhedral}, and $\Lam(x^*)\subsetneq\Lone(x^*)$ with a curved boundary. For instance $\lambda=(0.3,0,1)\in\Lone\setminus\Lam$: the point $(0.55,0,1)$ improves $W_\lambda$ by $0.10125$. The degeneracy $F\equiv$ const is inessential: subtracting $\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}x_i^2$ from each own payoff makes $F$ $\varepsilon$-strongly monotone, keeps $x^*$ for $\varepsilon<1$, and the boundary of the slice remains curved (E2c in Section~\ref{sec:exp}, numerically). \status{proved here; the counterexample was pointed out in the internal review of v0.1}
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Two commodities: $\Lam\ne\Lone$ in routing]\label{ex:routing}
Theorem~\ref{thm:cone}(a),(b) use neither the Nash property nor the product structure: they hold for any finite family of $C^1$ functions $u_k$ on $K$ and any $x^*\in K$, with $\Lam$ and $\Lone$ defined by the same formulas. Apply them to the costs $C_k(f)=\sum_{p\in P_k}f_pc_p(f)$ borne by the commodities of a network, $u_k=-C_k$, at a Wardrop equilibrium $f^*$. Take two commodities with unit demands: commodity 1 chooses between a private edge of cost $f+2$ and a shared edge of cost $f$; commodity 2 between a private edge of cost $f/4$ and the same shared edge. With $y=(f_B,f_D)\in[0,1]^2$ the flows sent to the shared edge (path flows are determined by edge flows here, so $f^*$ is a well-defined point),
\[
C_1(y)=(1-f_B)(3-f_B)+f_B(f_B+f_D),\qquad C_2(y)=\tfrac14(1-f_D)^2+f_D(f_B+f_D),
\]
both with indefinite Hessians (determinant $-1$, from the shared-edge cross term), so (c) is unavailable. The Wardrop equilibrium is $f^*=(1,0)$ (shared path cost $1\le 2$ for commodity 1, private cost $\tfrac14\le 1$ for commodity 2), unique because the reduced operator $y\mapsto(c_B-c_A,\,c_D-c_C)$ has Jacobian $\begin{psmallmatrix}2&1\\1&5/4\end{psmallmatrix}\succ 0$; $C(f^*)=(1,\tfrac14)$. At $f^*$, $\NC_K(f^*)=\R_+\times\R_-$, $\nabla C_1(f^*)=(0,1)$ and $\nabla C_2(f^*)=(0,\tfrac12)$, so $-\lambda_1\nabla C_1-\lambda_2\nabla C_2=(0,-\lambda_1-\tfrac12\lambda_2)\in\NC_K(f^*)$ for every $\lambda\ge 0$: $\Lone(f^*)=\R^2_+$. For $\Lam$, fix $\lambda_2=1$ ($\lambda_2=0$ gives $\lambda\in\Lam$: $C_1$ is minimised at $f^*$). The Hessian of $\lambda_1C_1+C_2$ is $\begin{psmallmatrix}4\lambda_1&\lambda_1+1\\\lambda_1+1&5/2\end{psmallmatrix}$, positive semidefinite iff $10\lambda_1\ge(\lambda_1+1)^2$, in which case the first-order condition suffices and $\lambda\in\Lam$. Otherwise the minimum over the box lies on an edge, where the restriction is a convex quadratic: on $f_B=1$ and on $f_D=0$ it is attained at $f^*$ (derivatives $\lambda_1+\tfrac12+\tfrac52t>0$ and $4\lambda_1(f_B-1)\le 0$); on $f_D=1$ the value is at least $\tfrac{15}{8}\lambda_1+1>\lambda_1+\tfrac14$; on $f_B=0$ it is $3\lambda_1+\tfrac14(1-t)^2+t^2$, minimised at $t=\tfrac15$ with value $3\lambda_1+\tfrac15$. Hence $f^*$ is optimal iff $\lambda_1+\tfrac14\le3\lambda_1+\tfrac15$, and
\[
\Lam(f^*)=\{\lambda\ge 0:\lambda_2\le 40\lambda_1\}\subsetneq\Lone(f^*)=\R^2_+ .
\]
For $\lambda=(0,1)$, the flow $y=(0,\tfrac15)$ reroutes commodity 1 to its private edge and sends a fifth of commodity 2 through the shared edge, lowering $C_2$ from $\tfrac14$ to $\tfrac15$ (while $C_1$ rises from $1$ to $3$). The first-order cone misses this because $f^*$ is a corner at which the KKT conditions hold for every weighting, while the per-commodity costs are non-convex in path flows. ($f^*$ is also the utilitarian optimum.) \status{proved here; verified in E5}
\end{example}

\section{Limitations and open questions}\label{sec:limits}

\paragraph{Limitations.}
(1) Everything is finite-dimensional, deterministic and $C^1$; mixed strategies, atomic congestion games, dynamics and stochastic demand are outside the note. (2) Theorem~\ref{thm:cone} is a first-order statement; its only non-trivial sufficient condition for equality is concavity, which excludes Cournot-type payoffs and multi-commodity routing costs. (3) The numerical experiments verify theorems on quadratic games over boxes and on small networks; the reported frequencies describe one sampling distribution and carry no general meaning. (4) Deciding $\lambda\in\Lam(x^*)$ for a non-concave quadratic $W_\lambda$ on a box is a non-convex quadratic programme, tractable here only because $N\le 4$. (5) We have not surveyed the toll-pricing and multi-objective literature exhaustively (Remark~\ref{rem:novelty}).

\begin{question}[Structure of $\Lam(x^*)$ beyond first order]\label{q:second}
For polynomial payoffs on a polyhedral $K$, $\Lam(x^*)=\{\lambda\ge 0:\forall x\in K,\ W_\lambda(x)\le W_\lambda(x^*)\}$ is defined by a first-order formula over the reals, hence is semialgebraic by the Tarski--Seidenberg theorem~\citep{bochnakCosteRoy1998}: a finite description by polynomial inequalities always exists (for quadratic payoffs on a box, $\Lam=\bigcap_\Phi\{\lambda:W_\lambda(x^*)\ge\max_\Phi W_\lambda\}$ over the $3^N$ faces $\Phi$, the parabola of Example~\ref{ex:nonpoly} being the term of the face $\{x_2=0,x_3=1\}$), and for $N=2$ every closed convex cone of $\R^2$ is polyhedral. Example~\ref{ex:nonpoly} shows that for $N=3$ the cone need not be polyhedral. What remains: (i) characterise, in terms of the active-face pattern of $x^*$ and the Hessians, when $\Lam(x^*)=\Lone(x^*)$ and when $\Lam(x^*)$ is polyhedral without joint concavity; (ii) determine the complexity of deciding $\lambda\in\Lam(x^*)$ for quadratic payoffs on a box (it contains the certification that $x^*$ is a global maximiser of an indefinite quadratic over the box; already deciding local optimality of a point in an indefinite quadratic programme is NP-hard \citep{murtyKabadi1987}), and whether a description of size polynomial in $N$ exists when $F$ is strongly monotone.
\end{question}

\begin{question}[Commodity weights in routing]\label{q:multi}
In a multi-commodity network let $C_k(f)=\sum_{p\in P_k}f_pc_p(f)$ and let $f^*$ be a Wardrop equilibrium \emph{in path flows}; edge flows are unique for strictly increasing costs but path flows are not, and $\Lam(f^*)$ depends on the representative chosen. Theorem~\ref{thm:cone}(a),(b) give $\Lam(f^*)\subseteq\Lone(f^*)$, with equality when no edge is shared between commodities (each $C_k$ is then convex for affine costs), and Example~\ref{ex:routing} shows that $\Lam\ne\Lone$ already for two commodities, one shared edge and affine costs. Characterise $\Lam(f^*)$ for $m\ge 3$ commodities and affine costs (by analogy with Example~\ref{ex:nonpoly} we expect it to be non-polyhedral in general) and identify the network structures for which $\Lam=\Lone$.
\end{question}

\paragraph{Solver.} Projection onto the box is coordinatewise clipping; projection onto the simplex uses the sort-based exact algorithm. The step is $\gamma=\mu/L^2$ with $\mu$ the least eigenvalue of the symmetric part of the Jacobian and $L$ its spectral norm ($\mu=\min a_e$, $L=\max a_e$ for parallel links); the iteration stops when the step is below $10^{-12}$ (E1) or $10^{-13}$ (E2--E5). In Example~\ref{ex:nonpoly} $F$ is constant ($\mu=L=0$) and the fixed-point map with $\gamma=1$ is used; the residual at $x^*$ certifies the equilibrium.
\paragraph{Face enumeration.} For each of the $3^N$ patterns in $\{0,1,\text{free}\}^N$ the free coordinates solve $H_{ff}x_f=h_f-H_{fb}x_b$; candidates outside $[-10^{-9},1+10^{-9}]$ are discarded; the maximum over candidates is the maximum over the box, because a maximiser lies in the relative interior of some face and is stationary for the restriction. If $H_{ff}$ is singular the system is either inconsistent (no stationary point in the relative interior of that face, so the maximum over the face is attained on a lower-dimensional face, also enumerated) or consistent with $W_\lambda$ constant along the null directions (the same value is attained on a lower-dimensional face); the least-squares candidate used in that case is therefore redundant and the enumeration remains exhaustive.
\paragraph{Boundary detection.} A coordinate within $10^{-9}$ of $0$ or $1$ is active (the projection returns exact bounds).

\section{Protocols and criteria}\label{app:protocols}
\paragraph{E1.} E1a: Example~\ref{ex:pigou} in closed form and by a grid search over the split ($2\times10^5$ points). E1b: \AffInstances{} parallel-link networks with $2$--$5$ links, $a_e\sim U(0.05,1)$, $b_e=0$ with probability $\tfrac12$ and otherwise $U(0,1)$, unit demand; equilibrium ($F=c$) and optimum ($F=\nabla C=2a\circ f+b$) by the solver on the simplex and, independently, by exact water-filling on the common (marginal) cost level. E1c: the tolled operator $c+\tau$, $\tau_e=a_ef_e$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:toll}), by the solver, against the water-filling optimum. \emph{Criteria:} residuals $\le10^{-8}$; solver vs.\ water-filling $\le10^{-7}$; PoA $\le4/3+10^{-9}$ in every instance and $4/3$ to $10^{-12}$ for Pigou with $d=1$; tolled equilibrium vs.\ water-filling optimum $\le10^{-7}$.

\paragraph{E2--E4.} \emph{Games:} $N$ players, scalar actions in $[0,1]$, $u_i(x)=-\tfrac12x^{\!\top}H_ix+h_i^{\!\top}x$, so $F(x)=Mx-m$ with $M_{ij}=(H_i)_{ij}$; concave family $H_i=B_iB_i^{\!\top}$ with Gaussian $B_i$, non-concave family $H_i$ Gaussian symmetric; in both, the diagonals are raised by a common amount so that the symmetric part of $M$ has least eigenvalue $\tfrac12$ (strong monotonicity, unique equilibrium, own-concavity); $h_i\sim N(1,1.5^2)$, which puts \RndNFourCBdry--\RndNThreeXBdry\% of the equilibrium coordinates on the boundary. \emph{Tests:} $\lambda\in\Lone$ is the linear system of Theorem~\ref{thm:cone}(d) with $\NC_{[0,1]}(0)=\R_-$, $\NC_{[0,1]}(1)=\R_+$ and $\{0\}$ inside, checked to $10^{-9}$; $\lambda\in\Lam$ iff $W_\lambda(x^*)\ge\max_KW_\lambda-10^{-8}$, with the maximum by face enumeration (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}), cross-checked against multistart projected gradient ascent on \RndPgaChecks{} concave instances (discrepancy \RndPgaDiff); a point with $W_\lambda(x)>W_\lambda(x^*)+10^{-6}$ is a \emph{certified witness}. \emph{Geometry of $\Lone$:} non-triviality and dimension by linear programming (implicit equalities row by row); up to $12$ vertices of $\Lone\cap\{\mathbf 1^{\!\top}\lambda=1\}$ plus their barycentre are the \emph{cone points}; every point of a simplex grid (step $1/12$ for $N=3$, $1/8$ for $N=4$) is classified by both tests. \emph{Criteria:} (E2) closed form $=$ linear $=$ exact on all \TwoAngles{} directions of Example~\ref{ex:wedge}; no grid direction in $\Lam$ for Example~\ref{ex:cournot}(i); linear $=$ exact on all grid directions for (ii); $\max W'_\lambda\le10^{-12}$ on a $\NIgrid\times\NIgrid$ grid of $K$ and \NIlambdas{} directions for Proposition~\ref{prop:notdet}. (E2c) exact test $=$ closed form of Example~\ref{ex:nonpoly} on a $121\times61$ grid of the slice $\lambda_3=1$; bisected boundary within $10^{-7}$ of the parabola; $(0.3,0,1)\in\Lone\setminus\Lam$ with gap $0.10125$ to $10^{-9}$. (E3) every cone point and every grid point in $\Lone$ is in $\Lam$, every grid point outside $\Lone$ is outside $\Lam$. (E4) the latter still holds (Theorem~\ref{thm:cone}(b)); witnesses are counted with no prediction. All residuals $\le10^{-8}$.

\paragraph{E5.} The network of Example~\ref{ex:routing} and \CmRandInst{} random instances of the same shape ($a\sim U(0.05,1)$; $b=0$ with probability $\tfrac12$, else $U(0,1)$; unit demands), solved by the projected-gradient iteration for the reduced operator on $[0,1]^2$; $\Lone$ by the linear test and $\Lam$ by face enumeration of $\lambda_1C_1+\lambda_2C_2$ on \CmDirs{} directions $\lambda=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$. \emph{Criteria:} residuals $\le10^{-8}$; fixed instance: all directions in $\Lone$, boundary ratio $1/40$ to $10^{-6}$, witness gap $1/20$ to $10^{-9}$ for $\lambda=(0,1)$; random instances: no direction certified in $\Lam$ (gap $\le10^{-11}$) outside $\Lone$. Directions accepted by the exact test only within its $10^{-8}$ tolerance while failing the linear test are recorded as tolerance-level disagreements (\CmRandTolDis{} in the reference run, largest first-order violation \CmRandTolDisViol).
